# Prologue
On the day the great black sphere descended upon Earth, humanity saw a god for the first time.
Fifty years later, humanity had limitless energy.
A hundred years later, humanity left the solar system.
Another two hundred years passed, and humanity became capable of migration on a stellar scale.
Historians would come to call that day—
“the Revelation.”

# 01
When the last Kexian fell, Isaac wearily slid his hunting knife back into its sheath.

The black blood spattered over him was sticky and foul-smelling, and he hated the feel of it. All he wanted now was to get back to his cocoon, take a long hot shower, and knock back a few good mouthfuls of liquor.

“Could do with a cigarette.”

Isaac muttered it to himself and gave the corpse beside him another kick.

The body rolled, exposing its tail. Isaac perked up at once. He crouched, took out a small knife, and turned the corpse over. With practiced movements, he slit the skin above the tail, removed a section of tailbone, and slipped it into the pouch at his waist.

The pouch had cost him a full three time credits, but at least it was sturdy and had plenty of room inside.

Isaac hefted the heavy pouch and counted in his head.

“Must be over two hundred. Filthy bastards, but their bones are worth something.”

The Empire had paid handsomely to hire them to put down this rebellion. A section of Kexian tailbone fetched ten time credits. More than two hundred pieces of bone would bring in more than two thousand credits.

That meant Isaac could live another two thousand years.

The thought took much of the weariness out of him.

Whistling, Isaac walked toward the mercenary ship parked in the distance.

It was less a spaceship than an enormous insect shaped like a balloon. People called it a broodship. These creatures could travel through space at close to the speed of light, their bodies filled with viscous interstitial fluid. During the voyage, passengers had to remain completely submerged in the fluid to withstand the pressure of acceleration. Otherwise, they would be crushed to pulp in an instant.

Before entering the interstitial fluid, Isaac looked through the broodship’s translucent body for one last glimpse of Kexia below.

He seemed to be admiring the planet. Or perhaps the deaths of the thousands upon thousands of Kexians on it.

Kexia lay at the edge of one of the Milky Way’s spiral arms. It was cold and damp all year round, with almost no native life except a species of subterranean rat.

To survive, the early settlers had combined their bodies with those rats, eventually becoming the Kexians of today—or, as the people of the Empire called them, Kexian rats.

They had human heads and rat bodies. Crouching, they stood only half as tall as an ordinary human, but on their hind legs they could almost look an adult in the eye. After so many years underground, their eyes had atrophied, while the hair covering their bodies had been modified into exquisitely sensitive sensory organs. Even in total darkness, minute vibrations in the air and ground told them everything about their surroundings.

Recently, the Kexians had petitioned the Empire for permission to leave their homeworld.

The Empire had refused.

So they had rebelled.

Now the rebellion was over.

Back home, Isaac threw his coat, slimy with black blood, onto the floor and sank into the sofa. He unscrewed the bottle and took a few contented swallows of whisky.

Over humanity’s long history, vast numbers of natural plants had either gone extinct or been altered into something else. This ancient liquor and the method of making it, however, had stubbornly survived.

Isaac had always considered that one of the few things in human history genuinely worth celebrating.

Then someone knocked at the door.

Isaac closed his eyes and pretended not to hear.

The knocking came again, harder this time.

“Fucking bastards!”

He swore angrily, but hauled himself off the sofa all the same.

A short, fat, middle-aged man stood outside.

“Vulture. How are you not dead yet? Those bastards in the Inspectorate actually let you go?”

“As long as there are time credits in it for them, the Inspectorate’s open to negotiation.” The fat man beamed.

“Cut the shit, Vulture. What do you want? I’ve got nothing to sell you.” Isaac was impatient. Nothing annoyed him more than being disturbed while he was resting.

“There’s a big job. Pull it off and it’s worth fifty thousand time credits. My Creator! That’s longer than the Empire’s been around. We split it seventy-thirty. Seventy for you, thirty for me. What do you say?”

Isaac looked at Vulture for a few seconds, then tipped back his head and drained his glass.

“Fifty thousand time credits?” He wiped his mouth. “If you really had something worth that much, you wouldn’t be standing at my door. You’d be lying in your cocoon, celebrating the fact you’ll live to see the galaxy go dark.”

“Which is why I came to you.” Vulture squeezed into the room and closed the door behind him. “It’s more than I can handle alone.”

“Tell me what it is first.”

“A ship.”

“Get out.”

Isaac dropped back onto the sofa, not even bothering to put down his glass.

Hundreds of derelict ships were discovered within the Empire every day. Some were smugglers’ vessels, some were transport broodships whose owners were gone, and some were merely large organisms that had died in space. Their organs would be stripped out and made into new engines, their bones ground down for hull material, and the remaining meat sent to the culture vats. With luck, they might fetch a few hundred time credits.

But fifty thousand?

Only if the Creator himself was lying inside.

“It’s on Planet-127JX.” Vulture stayed where he was. “A mining crew dug it up.”

“A mining crew digs up a wreck, and you’re going to pay me fifty thousand time credits to take a look?”

“It was buried more than four thousand meters underground.”

Isaac raised his eyelids and looked at him.

Vulture went to the table, took a thumb-sized flesh sac from his clothes, and set it down. At the touch of warmth, the sac slowly unfurled. The membrane on its surface split apart, projecting a blurred recording into the air.

The image showed a vast excavated cavity underground.

Countless searchlights shone toward the center of the cavern, where a black mothership lay embedded at an angle in the rock. Most of its hull was still buried beneath earth and stone; only the bow and a small section of its flank were exposed. Unlike the bloated, soft-bodied, insectlike ships of the present, this thing still had the shape of an old human warship.

Long, hard, bristling with gunports.

Only it was no longer made of steel.

Dark red muscle tissue grew along the outer hull, thrusting like vines into cracks in the armor. Bones jutted from beneath the belly, supporting the entire mothership. Metal and flesh had long since grown together, with no gaps between them. It was nothing like the broodships of later ages, grown entirely from living tissue. It looked more like an old human warship that some force had gradually assimilated from within.

“What model is it?” Isaac asked.

“No idea.”

“Imperial?”

“No.”

Vulture shifted the image with a finger. Beside the hull stood a miner, no bigger than a fingernail.

“No serial number we can find. No Imperial insignia. No broodship hatchery markings. The mining crew’s appraiser analyzed the hull structure. The outermost layer is titanium alloy, with a kind of ceramic armor that went out of use long ago. The technology is at least thirteen thousand years old.”

Isaac frowned.

“The Empire was only founded thirteen thousand five hundred and seventy-six years ago.”

“That’s why some people think it was built before the Empire.” Vulture grinned. “It might even date from the early days of the Revelation.”

Isaac studied the image for a while, then quickly lost interest again.

“So what? A pile of antiques wrapped in meat. A museum might give you a few hundred credits, assuming you can dig it out.”

“If it were just an antique, I wouldn’t be here.”

Vulture spread two fat, stubby fingers and enlarged the image. A patch of blackened flesh on the hull filled the projection. It looked long dead and desiccated, its surface covered in rock and dust.

Then the flesh slowly swelled.

And sank.

A few seconds later, it swelled again.

Dust around the entire hull trembled with it.

Isaac sat up.

“What’s that?”

“A pulse.”

The room fell silent except for the dull pounding in the recording.

Thud.

Thud.

Thud.

Each beat seemed to come from deep beneath the ground. In the recording, the miners stopped working. They all stood motionless, looking at the mothership that had been buried for more than ten thousand years.

“When the crew first found it, they thought it was movement in the rock.” Vulture lowered his voice. “Then they put sensors against the hull and found the sound was coming from inside the mothership. The closer they got to the bridge, the stronger the pulse.”

“Something still alive in there?”

“Nobody knows.”

“They didn’t go in?”

“The first group never came out.”

Isaac looked at Vulture.

The fat man was still smiling, though the smile had stiffened a little.

“The second group only made it to the first hatch. No console, no switch. The whole door was clamped shut like a mouth. They burned at it with cutters for three hours. Didn’t leave a mark. Then someone sent a probe insect inside.”

“And?”

“The probe died.”

“An old ship with a defense system. What’s so strange about that?”

“It wasn’t a weapon that killed it.” Vulture paused. “The moment it crawled into a crack, all the dried-out flesh around the mothership woke up at once. It caught the probe and dragged it into the hull, bit by bit. A few minutes later, a new eye grew on the ship.”

The image changed again.

On the flank of the black hull, a cloudy eyeball sat embedded between armor and muscle. It was nearly the size of a hatch, with a fine mesh of blood vessels growing around it. The eye turned slowly, until it looked straight at whoever was making the recording.

Even through the projection, Isaac felt it was looking at him.

“How many of the crew died?” he asked.

“Fourteen.”

“And the rest?”

“Sealed off the pit. Waiting for the Empire to send someone to take over.”

“And you still want to go?”

“Once the Empire gets there, we won’t get so much as a bone.”

Isaac shut off the projection and tossed the flesh sac from the table back into Vulture’s arms.

“I just got back from Kexia. Killed over two hundred rats and made seven hundred and eighty credits. I’m not short of time, and I don’t fancy crawling into some man-eating wreck. Find someone else.”

“Seven hundred and eighty credits?” Vulture glanced down at the coat on the floor. Black blood was dripping from its hem. “You spent four months on a shithole of a planet for seven hundred and eighty years. Now all you have to do is come along with me, and you get thirty-five thousand.”

“If I make it back alive.”

“Since when were you afraid of dying?”

“I’m not afraid of dying.” Isaac poured himself another drink. “I only just bought this pouch. Haven’t got my money’s worth yet.”

Vulture stopped trying to persuade him and took something else from inside his clothes.

It was a grayish-white metal plate, no bigger than a palm, with a rim of dead flesh around its edges. A few blurred characters were engraved on the metal, apparently in some early human script.

Isaac paid it little attention until he saw the design at its center.

A black sphere encircled by countless fine lines.

A long, narrow slit opened at the center of the sphere, with a golden eye engraved inside it.

Everyone in the Empire knew that image.

The Creator.

But it was not the Creator’s holy emblem as it appeared in churches, on warships, or in time banks. There was no halo, no kneeling multitude, none of the spears representing the Thirteen Saints of the Empire.

It simply hung there in silence.

Like a great black sphere that had just descended upon Earth.

“Peeled this off the hull?” Isaac asked.

“No,” said Vulture. “It fell off by itself.”

“What do you mean?”

“After the miners took it out of the pit, the mothership’s heart stopped for a full three minutes. When they brought it back, the heart started again.”

Vulture set the metal plate on the table.

The grayish-white surface suddenly began to tremble. The dead tissue around its edges twitched like tiny worms. An exceedingly fine blood vessel emerged and pierced the tabletop.

Thud.

A faint beat sounded in the room.

Isaac stared at the metal plate, his glass suspended halfway to his mouth.

“Did you hear that?” Vulture asked.

“I’m not deaf yet.”

“The pit is seventy-six light-years from here.” The smile had left Vulture’s face. “But wherever you take this thing, its heartbeat stays in time with the mothership’s.”

Thud.

Thud.

The metal plate pulsed twice more.

Isaac was silent for a long time. At last he drained his glass.

“When do we leave?”

Vulture smiled again.

“Now.”

“I’m taking a shower first.”

“No time.”

“Why?”

Vulture glanced out the window.

Far off in the night sky, several enormous Imperial patrol insects were moving slowly through the clouds. Red lights blinked in their abdomens. Pale searchlight organs swept the city, like a row of eyes hunting for prey.

“Because the Empire’s heard its heartbeat too.”

# 02

Isaac slept inside the broodship for eighty years.

For people in the old days, eighty years had been almost an entire lifetime. Birth, growing up, growing old, and finally being packed into a small box: that was about how long it took.

For humanity now, it was merely a slightly longer sleep.

When Isaac woke, the broodship was draining its interstitial fluid.

The viscous liquid slowly receded through the bottom of the chamber. Naked on a soft membrane of flesh, he coughed violently and spat the last few mouthfuls of rank fluid from his lungs.

A transparent tendril descended from above and slid into his ear.

“Voyage duration: eighty-one standard years.”

“Destination: Planet-127JX.”

“Current lifespan loss: zero point seven time credits.”

Isaac yanked the tendril out.

“Charging me in my sleep now.”

The wall of flesh beside him slowly parted, revealing the sky outside.

127JX was a terrestrial planet.

It had vast natural oceans, a stable atmosphere, and a climate that, after thousands of years of adjustment, almost never underwent violent changes. Gravity was only three-quarters of Earth’s. Most locals stood half a head taller than people from Earth, with long limbs but sturdy bones. After generations of genetic refinement, they were well adapted to the environment.

The planet’s cities were divided into two parts: aboveground and below.

The surface belonged to the rich, the legislators, the corporate representatives, and those who claimed they simply preferred sunlight. They lived among mountains and forests, the flesh of their buildings trimmed neat and pretty. Not a single exposed blood vessel showed on the outer walls.

Ordinary people lived underground.

The cities descended hundreds of kilometers through the rock, countless homes, factories, and culture vats crowded together. Blood flowed through the pipes day and night, carrying energy, oxygen, and formulated nutrient solutions to the inhabitants below.

Even so, 127JX was still considered one of the most habitable planets in the galaxy.

At least the air did not make your lungs grow mold, and things rarely crawled out of the sewers at night to eat people.

127JX enjoyed an exceptional degree of autonomy.

The local assembly did not welcome outsiders. Residency permits were absurdly expensive, and applications usually took decades to process. As a result, the permanent population had remained steady at around four billion for a long time. Plenty of people were willing to spend half a lifetime’s time credits on a place to live, only to find they could not even get into the port.

Isaac, however, took less than two hours.

At the first checkpoint, a long, thin bone needle pierced his finger and drew a drop of blood.

At the second, several soft tendrils crawled over his face, inspecting his pupils, teeth, and skull.

At the third, a guard told him to put his entire hand into a mouth full of teeth.

The mouth closed around his hand. A tongue circled his palm.

Text appeared on the wall of flesh beside him.

Mining crew, Extraction Group Seven.

Deep-level equipment maintenance technician.

Remaining term of employment: one hundred and thirty-six years.

Identity confirmed.

The checkpoint slowly opened.

Isaac pulled out his saliva-covered hand and wiped it on his clothes.

Somehow, Vulture had added his genetic sequence to the mining crew’s personnel records. Now the port, city transit, and mine management systems would all treat him as a veteran miner who had worked there for decades.

Nobody asked why he had come.

Nobody checked his weapons.

The guard could not even be bothered to give him a second look.

Isaac had to admit that Vulture’s thirty percent was not entirely down to sheer nerve.

Vulture himself, of course, had not come.

A merchant would send anyone into danger for a profit, but never himself.

If Vulture had actually spent eighty years asleep aboard the broodship and then come in person to this man-eating mothership, Isaac would have begun to doubt whether the fifty thousand time credits were real.

Once through the checkpoints, Isaac went straight to 127JX’s transit tubes.

A dense network of transparent tubes ran beneath the entire city, like blood vessels buried in the rock. The walls periodically split open to admit passengers. Once everyone was standing securely, the opening would heal, and clear liquid would rush in to flush them toward their destinations.

Isaac approached one of the tubes and pressed his palm against its wall.

Countless cilia, all but invisible to the naked eye, settled against his skin and read his genetic sequence. Once his identity as a member of the mining crew had been confirmed, the soft wall contracted to either side, opening a gap just wide enough for one person.

The liquid inside had already drained away. Only a damp membrane remained, slowly rising and falling against the inner wall.

Several bounty hunters who had traveled with him were already waiting there.

They too were working for Vulture, and they too had legitimate identities as members of the mining crew. No one asked how many time credits Vulture had promised the others.

Best not to ask about that sort of thing.

It tended to get a few people killed before there was any money to divide.

They climbed into the tube one after another. As soon as the last person stepped inside, the opening behind them healed without a seam.

The walls slowly tightened, holding everyone in place at the center.

A moment later, a muffled roar of water came from ahead.

A mass of transparent liquid surged from deep inside the tube and engulfed them. Before Isaac could close his mouth, the current swept him up and hurled him down through the tube toward the depths.

The city flashed past beyond the translucent walls.

They passed rows of homes grown into the rock, traveled through factories wrapped in blood vessels, then crossed beneath an enormous culture vat. At each junction, the inner walls contracted to redirect the current, washing different passengers into different branches.

A dozen or so hours later, the current finally slowed.

The wall opened before them, and the clear liquid was rapidly drawn away. Isaac tumbled out along the slippery lining and landed on a receiving platform in an abandoned mining district.

He lay on the ground coughing and spat out a great mouthful of water.

“Next time I’d rather sleep another eighty years.”

The pit lay more than four thousand meters underground.

The surrounding rock had been hollowed out into a cavern whose far end was lost to sight. Hundreds of searchlights had been fixed to the rock walls, their pale beams crossing over the center of the cave.

The mothership was there.

It was even larger than it had looked in the recording.

The ship lay wedged at an angle in the rock, most of its hull still buried beneath earth and stone. The exposed section alone was several kilometers long. Its stern rose high, like the bare spine of some dead leviathan.

Dark red flesh covered almost all the armor.

In places it had shriveled and blackened, fusing with the rock. Elsewhere it was still wet, threaded with slowly contracting blood vessels. Several thick bones drove down from beneath the hull into the stone. It looked less as though the ship had crashed here than as though, over the long years, it had rooted itself firmly in the planet.

Thud.

A deep beat sounded inside the hull.

Loose stones trembled underfoot.

A few seconds passed. Then another.

Thud.

Isaac looked up at it.

He had fought in the Empire’s border wars. He had seen war broodships longer than a city. But however monstrous those things looked, at least you could tell they were tools that had been grown for a purpose.

This ship was different.

There was no clear boundary between its metal and its flesh.

It did not look as though flesh had overgrown the warship. It looked as though the warship itself had contracted a disease.

Several small transport insects were parked near the pit. More than twenty people were sorting weapons, lighting organs, and tools for cutting tissue. Some were bounty hunters recruited by Vulture. The rest were members of the mining crew that had first discovered the mothership.

No Imperial troops.

No Inspectorate.

Not even a trace of 127JX’s local security forces.

A miner approached Isaac. He was almost a head taller, his protective suit covered in grayish-white dust.

“You the leader Vulture hired?”

“That was the lie he told me.”

The miner offered his hand. Isaac ignored it and tipped his chin toward the mothership.

“How long has word been out?”

“Pretty much everyone who needs to know knows.” The miner lowered his hand. “Bounty hunters from the neighboring sectors are already on their way. The assembly here on 127JX is still arguing about who gets control. An Imperial vessel is on its way to investigate, too.”

“Then how come we’re still standing here?”

“Because we found it first.”

The miner glanced around and lowered his voice.

“And we work for Vulture.”

Isaac looked at the mothership again.

Eighty years ago, the moment the mining crew uncovered the first piece of armor, the recording had probably been sent to Vulture. By the time anyone else heard about it, his people, weapons, and identities were already on their way.

It occurred to Isaac that Vulture taking thirty percent might not be entirely unreasonable.

Not entirely. That was all.

“How much time do we have?” he asked.

“Two days, if things go well.”

“And if they don’t?”

The miner glanced up at the cavern roof.

“The Empire could show up at any moment.”

“Where’s the way in?”

The miner pointed to the port side of the hull.

“First group went in there. Never came out. Second group reached the first hatch and turned back. We can’t open the door or cut through the hull.”

“Internal layout?”

“None.”

“Life signs?”

“Everywhere.”

“Air?”

“Breathable.”

“That’ll do.”

Isaac took a hunting knife from the weapons crate and checked its edge, then picked up a bone pistol and tucked it into his belt.

He turned to the bounty hunters.

Some were checking their weapons. Some stared blankly at the mothership. Two were arguing over who should bring up the rear. None of them wanted to be the first to approach that still-beating thing.

Behind him, the miner said:

“Vulture said you were to lead them in.”

Isaac’s face darkened.

“That included in the seventy percent?”

“His exact words were, that’s mostly what the seventy percent is for.”

Isaac swore and started toward the mothership.

The others looked at one another, then finally followed him.

The entrance lay beneath a torn section of armor, like a wound that had not yet healed. Strips of congealed flesh curled back to either side, exposing a narrow black passage. Fragments of the crushed probe insect’s shell remained around the edges. A newly grown eye sat beside the opening, slowly turning as they approached.

Isaac stopped in front of the entrance.

The eye looked at him.

Deep in the hull, the heartbeat suddenly quickened.

Thud.

Isaac gripped his knife and ducked into the gap.

“Stay close.” His voice came out of the darkness. “You get lost, I’m not coming back for you.”

They entered the mothership one by one.

After the last of them crossed the threshold, the dried flesh on either side twitched and began slowly closing inward.

The strip of light from outside narrowed until it vanished.

In the darkness, the mothership’s heart beat again.

Thud.
# 03

Once inside the mothership, everyone stopped.

There was not a trace of light.

The searchlights outside the entrance reached only a few meters in. Their pale beams fell on the bulkheads to either side and were quickly swallowed by the dark red flesh. Farther in there was only dense blackness, as though nothing existed there at all.

A stale, bloody smell hung in the air.

The floor rose and fell slightly beneath their feet. At intervals, a dull heartbeat came from deep within the hull.

Thud.

It traveled through the soles of their feet and into their chests.

At the head of the group, Isaac extended two fingers and drove them straight into his right eye.

Before the people beside him could react, he had hooked the edge of the eyeball and pulled hard.

The wet sound of tearing tissue was very clear in the dark.

He pried the entire eyeball from its socket. Fine nerves still trailed behind it, twitching like white threads. Blood welled immediately from the empty socket and ran down his cheek to his chin.

Isaac tilted his head back and clenched his teeth.

“Sss—”

His whole face screwed up with pain. It took him a few seconds to catch his breath.

“Fuck.”

All the miners behind him were staring.

One of them reached instinctively for his own eye.

Isaac ignored them. He pinched through the nerves behind the eyeball with his thumbnail and put the eye in his mouth, then peeled the glove off his left arm.

Beneath the glove was nothing resembling normal skin.

From wrist to elbow, his arm was studded with closed eyes. Large and small, they crowded between muscle and blood vessels, each covered by a translucent membrane. As the glove came off, the eyes seemed to sense the disturbance. Their lids trembled all at once.

Isaac considered them for a moment, then pressed down on a grayish eye.

His fingers sank into the soft tissue around it.

He hooked it out. The eyeball came free of his arm, leaving a round hollow that kept contracting.

Isaac pressed it into his empty right socket.

The tiny nerves behind the eye grew rapidly, burrowing deep into the socket to join the severed nerves. The surrounding muscles writhed, then quickly closed tight around the eye.

He blinked a few times.

The pitch-black compartment began to take shape.

Then he spat the eye from his mouth into his hand and pushed it into the space he had just emptied in his left arm. The flesh closed immediately, swallowing the eyeball. A new eyelid slowly grew over it, leaving it indistinguishable from the other closed eyes.

Isaac reached for his left eye.

The miner behind him could no longer keep quiet.

“You’re doing that again?”

“What, keep one eye to look at the dark and one to see with?”

Isaac drove two fingers into his left eye.

Another tearing sound set their teeth on edge.

Several of the miners quietly looked away.

Once the second eye was in place, Isaac pulled his glove back on. The two gray eyeballs turned in their sockets. Their pupils rapidly dilated until they filled almost the entire eye.

His surroundings gradually came into focus.

The walls, the floor, and the tangled blood vessels overhead all appeared in dim gray-white. Farther away, patches of still-living tissue gave off faint heat, showing as blurred red in his vision.

Isaac wiped the blood from his face.

“Fuck. All this time, and it still feels horrible.”

He blinked his eyes, which had not yet fully adjusted.

“Next time I’m getting a pair that switches to night vision on its own.”

Only then did the miners behind him start turning on their headlamps.

Beams shot past Isaac and lit the passage ahead.

Isaac turned to look at them.

“You have lights?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you turn them on?”

The miner glanced at the glove on Isaac’s left arm.

“Didn’t get the chance.”

Isaac stared at him for a few seconds, swore under his breath, and started forward.

The passage was wide enough for several transport vehicles to travel abreast.

On either side, the old warship’s structure was still visible. Metal supports protruded from beneath the flesh. Parts of the walls bore remnants of markings long since rendered illegible. Thick blood vessels entwined the conduits and ran along the corridor into the dark.

They moved on.

The floor remained faintly warm underfoot. Whenever a heartbeat sounded deep within the hull, the surrounding blood vessels contracted in time, as though something were pumping blood into every corner of the mothership.

No one spoke.

Only footsteps and the clink of equipment echoed through the empty corridor.

After more than ten minutes, they reached the first hatch.

The door took up almost the entire passage.

It had presumably once been made of some dark metal. Now it was completely encased in thick muscle tissue. Several bones had grown across it, overlapping like bars placed there to lock it shut.

There was no console.

No switch.

Not even a seam they could fit a tool into.

A miner stepped forward and laid his hand against the door.

The flesh behind it gave a faint pulse.

Then nothing.

“This is it,” the miner said. “Three hours last time. Not a mark.”

A bounty hunter took out a bone cutter.

Its tip split open, exposing a row of rapidly vibrating teeth. He pressed it against the center of the hatch. A shrill grinding began at once.

Sparks showered from the door.

A few minutes later, he switched the cutter off.

All he had done was scorch a small patch of the surface. The flesh beneath writhed a few times. The charred tissue sloughed away on its own, exposing fresh, unblemished flesh.

Someone else took out a swollen acid sac and squeezed its corrosive fluid onto the door.

Green liquid trickled slowly down the hatch.

White smoke rose from the flesh, but it did not corrode. Instead, the fluid gradually seeped into the door until it was gone, as though the door had drunk it.

“Did it just get thicker?” someone asked.

No one answered.

They tried bone drills, oscillating tools, and heated cutting wire.

The hatch stayed shut.

One bounty hunter lost patience and raised his pistol, aiming at the center of the hatch.

Isaac shoved the muzzle down.

“Want to bring this place down on us?”

“Can’t just stand here forever.”

“Then go stand over there and shoot yourself in the head first. Maybe it likes idiots.”

The man stared at Isaac, but finally put his weapon away.

They spread out and resumed the search for the hatch controls.

Isaac moved slowly along the wall.

His night-vision eyes could see the faint heat within the flesh. Most of the tissue near the hatch lay dormant. Only a few slender blood vessels still carried a sluggish flow. They converged along the base of the wall, then vanished beneath a raised lump of tissue.

Isaac crouched and lifted the membrane covering it with the tip of his knife.

A long, thin white cilium lay curled inside.

It was no thicker than a hair, its surface ringed with exceedingly fine down. Sensing the approaching knife tip, it immediately recoiled deeper into the wall.

“Something here.”

Several miners gathered around him.

“Looks like a neural interface,” one said.

“Seen one before?”

“No.”

“Then how do you know?”

“Looks like one.”

Isaac glanced back at him.

“This how your crew does everything?”

“More or less.”

Isaac looked at the cilium again.

It was still swaying gently, its tip reaching toward his wrist now and then as though searching for something.

Isaac laid the hunting knife against his wrist.

The miner immediately realized what he meant to do.

“Wait. We don’t know what it’ll—”

The blade opened the skin.

Blood welled from the cut.

As though it had caught the scent, the white cilium sprang from the base of the wall and burrowed into Isaac’s wound.

Something cold pierced up his arm and into his body.

Everything before his eyes vanished.

His night-vision eyes had not stopped working.

The whole world was gone.

No walls. No miners. No mothership beneath his feet. He seemed to have been thrown into a darkness without limits, stripped of body, sound, and time. Only something immense remained, hidden deep in the dark.

Thud.

A heartbeat sounded.

It was heavier than any he had heard outside.

Thud.

The second beat came.

The darkness shook with it.

Isaac felt that the heart was not deep inside the mothership.

It seemed to be somewhere beyond the darkness itself.

With every beat, something in the dark came a little closer.

Thud.

The third beat sounded.

Isaac jerked his hand back.

The white cilium tore from the wound and writhed violently in the air. A drop of black fluid welled from the break, then the cilium withdrew swiftly into the wall.

Isaac stumbled back several steps.

His sight returned.

The searchlights hurt his eyes. The figures around him were blurred. He looked down at his wrist. A fine ring of black blood vessels had appeared around the wound.

“What did you see?” the miner asked.

Isaac did not answer.

He could still hear the heartbeat.

Not from deep inside the mothership.

From inside his own body.

“There’s another door here!”

Someone suddenly shouted in the distance.

Everyone turned and ran toward the voice.

Isaac stayed where he was, staring at the black vessels on his wrist. They lasted only a few seconds, then sank beneath his skin and vanished without a trace.

“Isaac!”

The miner was calling from farther away.

Only then did Isaac look up and follow.

At some point, a long, narrow slit had opened in what had been an unbroken wall of flesh.

Beyond the slit was more darkness.

It looked less like a hatch than a wound where the flesh had been forced apart. Tissue on either side kept contracting. The gap was slowly closing.

“Wasn’t here a minute ago,” a bounty hunter said.

Isaac went up to the slit.

He reached inside and felt cold metal structures beyond it.

“There’s a passage behind it.”

“Can we force it open?”

Isaac drew his hunting knife and drove it into the slit.

The blade sank into flesh.

Warm blood gushed from the wound and spattered him from head to foot.

The entire mothership shuddered.

Isaac gripped the hilt with both hands and dragged the blade down with all his strength.

Muscle and blood vessels tore in succession.

The gash widened rapidly. Blood poured down the wall and around their feet. A low tremor came from deep within the mothership, as though something that had slept for more than ten thousand years had finally felt pain.

“That’s enough!”

The miner shouted from behind him.

Isaac pulled out his knife, turned sideways, and squeezed through the opening.

He had barely found his footing when the flesh behind him contracted violently.

Severed muscles rejoined. Within seconds, the gash had healed completely. The marks left by his knife vanished, leaving only the blood on the floor still slowly writhing.

The others were shut out.

“Isaac!”

A muffled shout came through the wall of flesh.

Isaac rapped on it with his knife hilt.

“Can you hear me?”

No one answered.

A moment later, a terrible roar erupted from all around him.

It came from no single direction.

The walls, the floor, the ceiling: the entire space was making the sound. Isaac felt as though he were standing in some giant beast’s throat, everything around him contracting and convulsing with pain.

The hull began to shake violently.

Blood vessels thrashed along the walls. Black liquid sprayed from the cracks. The floor heaved beneath his feet until Isaac had to drive his knife into the wall just to keep himself upright.

The roaring lasted a full ten minutes.

Then, abruptly, everything fell silent.

The blood vessels stopped twitching.

The floor leveled out.

Only blood still fell from the hem of Isaac’s clothes, dripping onto the floor.

He pulled his knife free and looked back at the completely healed wall of flesh.

The way back was gone.

Isaac tapped the wall with his knife hilt.

“Vulture had better have those fifty thousand credits.”

He turned around.

Ahead lay a narrow corridor leading deeper into the mothership. There were no lights on either side, no visible hatches. Only slender white cilia protruded from the walls, slowly turning as he moved.

As though watching him.

Isaac tightened his grip on the hunting knife and walked alone into the dark.
